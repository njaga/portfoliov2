I know how to write code.

Professional emails.

Business proposals.

Whole articles that strangers can read on the internet.

But there are three-line messages I can spend an hour writing.

Deleting.

Writing again.

Before eventually locking my phone.

Not because I can't find the words.

Sometimes it's exactly because I've found them.

And I'm not sure I'm ready to send them.

## Three little dots

There's something strange about the way we talk these days.

You can watch someone typing.

Three little dots appear.

Disappear.

Come back.

Then nothing.

You'll never know what they meant to say.

Maybe a long explanation.

Maybe a terrible joke.

Maybe just, *forget it*.

Sometimes I wonder how many conversations changed direction because of a message that never arrived.

A word might have reassured someone.

A sentence might have hurt.

An explanation might have prevented weeks of misunderstanding.

But nobody read it.

And life carried on as though it had never existed.

Maybe that's the strangest part.

**We can have so much to say and still let someone believe we've got nothing to add.**

## What I meant to say

I think we all have a small collection of unfinished sentences.

*I wanted to tell you that...*

*Actually, that day...*

*I'm sorry for...*

*It meant something to me when...*

Fairly simple sentences.

No complicated vocabulary.

No need to find a particularly elegant turn of phrase.

Yet sometimes they're harder to write than a document several pages long.

Because they make us vulnerable.

Telling someone we miss them means accepting that they might not feel the same.

Apologizing means admitting we haven't always lived up to the image we had of ourselves.

Explaining that something hurt means admitting a word or a gesture mattered more than we wanted to show.

And asking for help...

I think that sentence deserves a paragraph of its own.

You can be perfectly capable of helping others and have no idea how to tell them you need help too.

So we reach for easier expressions.

*I'm fine.*

*Don't worry.*

*I've got this.*

Three phrases that can hold far more than they say.

## The perfect message

Sometimes I reread a message several times before sending it.

Not necessarily because it's important.

Sometimes it's just a professional habit spilling into everything else.

I check the tone.

The punctuation.

The order of the sentences.

Could this be misunderstood?

Am I being too direct?

Not direct enough?

Does that full stop make me sound angry?

You end up negotiating with a punctuation mark. 😅

But behind the search for just the right wording, there can be something else.

The urge to control the other person's reaction.

To find words that won't start an argument.

Words that won't invite an awkward answer.

Words that let us say something without really taking the risk of saying it.

The trouble is, no message can guarantee the reaction of the person receiving it.

You can write as carefully as possible and still be misunderstood.

You can also write clumsily and be understood perfectly.

**The words belong to us until we send them. Then they meet someone else's story.**

And I think that's sometimes what makes us hesitate.

## We'll talk about it

There are also the conversations we never even begin.

The ones we put off.

Because it's not the right time.

Because the other person is busy.

Because we're tired.

Because we don't want to ruin a good day.

So we tell ourselves:

*We'll talk about it.*

A useful phrase.

It lets you put a conversation away without having to finish it.

Except some conversations take up more space the longer we postpone them.

We replay what we wanted to say.

Imagine the replies.

Prepare arguments for an argument that hasn't happened.

Sometimes we even grow resentful toward someone over a conversation they know nothing about.

It's absurd.

And yet I understand very well how it happens.

Finding the courage to talk isn't always easy.

Especially when the person facing us matters.

We often imagine that silence comes from indifference.

I think some silences come from exactly the opposite.

## Not everything deserves to be sent

But I don't want to celebrate every message we hold back.

Some drafts should probably never leave the phone.

The one written in anger.

The one more interested in hurting than explaining.

The one written just to have the last word.

Or the one we'd be embarrassed to read after calming down.

I know the feeling.

Write a reply.

Think it's particularly well put.

Almost brilliant.

Then read it a few minutes later and wonder why you were so determined to send it.

Thank goodness for the delete button.

In those moments, leaving a message as a draft isn't cowardice.

Maybe it's what stops us from adding a bad sentence to an already difficult situation.

But there's a difference between staying quiet to avoid causing unnecessary pain and staying quiet because you're never brave enough to be honest.

I'm not always sure where that line is.

## What we keep

When I build an application, I can revisit an older version.

Compare changes.

Recover a deleted line.

Understand what I was trying to do.

Human relationships don't come with version history.

There's no folder containing every sentence we wanted to say.

Every time we thought of someone without telling them.

Every thank-you we postponed.

Every explanation we rehearsed in our heads before deciding it wasn't worth it anymore.

Perhaps that's for the best.

I wouldn't want someone to read everything I've written and deleted.

They'd find some sincere thoughts.

Others, not so much.

Passing worries.

Sentences that were true for only five minutes.

And a few words I probably should have sent.

Those last ones interest me most.

The simple words.

The ones that didn't need a long explanation.

*Thank you.*

*I'm sorry.*

*I miss you.*

*I thought of you.*

We sometimes imagine we need a special occasion to say these things.

But I wonder how many ordinary occasions we miss while waiting for the right one.

## Save as draft

I don't know whether I'll become someone who always says what's on his mind.

I don't even want to.

Some thoughts need time.

Others don't need a recipient.

And some silences deserve to be respected.

But I'd like to learn to recognize the words that should leave the draft folder.

Not wait for perfect phrasing before thanking someone.

Not let a little pride stretch a distance longer than necessary.

Not assume people already know what they mean to us.

I'll probably keep writing messages I never send.

Hesitating before conversations.

Deleting a sentence after reading it ten times.

And perhaps this article is proof of that.

After all, it's easier to write publicly about the words we hold back than to address them to the person who should hear them.

It's an odd contradiction.

I could try to resolve it with a beautiful conclusion.

But I think I'll just close this article.

There might be a message I ought to send.

**And for once, I already know the words.**
