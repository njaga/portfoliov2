I accepted.

At least, that's what the website says.

All I wanted was to read an article.

A window appeared.

“We respect your privacy.”

Reassuring.

Right below it was a large button: **Accept all**.

Beside it, a quieter link: *Customize*.

I clicked.

Another window.

Then a list of settings I had never asked to explore.

Eventually, I accepted.

Not because I agreed with everything.

Because I wanted to read the article.

Somewhere, a box was probably checked.

*User consented.*

That's one way to tell the story.

## The choice was there

Technically, I could have refused.

That's often the answer when people criticize this kind of interface.

The button existed.

It wasn't invisible.

You just had to look for it, open the settings, and confirm your decision.

Nothing impossible.

But the accept button was right in front of me.

One second versus several.

In an interface, that difference matters.

You can give someone two choices while making one of them more work.

You're not taking away the choice.

You're putting it on a slope.

Imagine a staircase beside an escalator.

Both get you upstairs.

But if almost everyone chooses the escalator, it doesn't necessarily mean they hate stairs.

Sometimes they just take the route someone has made easiest.

Online, the easiest route is often the one that suits the service.

## The color of a button

We rarely notice how many decisions a page has already made for us.

Button size.

Color.

Placement.

The order of the options.

The words used to describe them.

“Continue” doesn't feel the same as “Confirm purchase.”

“Maybe later” tells a different story from “No, thanks.”

And “Enjoy the offer” sounds friendlier than “Start a paid subscription.”

None of these phrases physically forces anyone to click.

But they shape how we understand our options.

There's even a name for interfaces designed to nudge people into actions they might not otherwise choose: *dark patterns*, sometimes called deceptive design.

The name sounds dramatic.

The mechanism can be wonderfully ordinary.

One obvious button.

Another that looks like a footnote.

**We don't lose the freedom to decide. Some decisions simply become more tiring than others.**

## The middle plan

Consider another example.

You're looking for a subscription.

Three plans appear.

The first is affordable, but it's missing two features you need.

The third is expensive enough to make you hesitate.

And the middle one comes with a small badge.

*Most popular.*

Or better yet:

*Recommended for you.*

For me?

We've barely met.

I'm not saying every recommendation is dishonest.

Sometimes the middle plan really is the best fit for most people.

Sometimes the recommendation also serves a business goal.

The difficulty is that both can look exactly the same.

The reader sees advice.

The company sees a conversion rate.

Those aren't necessarily the same definition of success.

You notice similar details everywhere: an extra option already selected, a free trial that becomes paid, notifications enabled from the first launch, a suggested item moments before checkout.

Each screen can seem harmless on its own.

Together, they begin to shape how we behave.

## I'm on the other side too

I work in tech.

I build websites and applications. I think about user journeys, buttons, forms, and ways to make interfaces easier to use.

I know how much a small detail can change an experience.

Move an action.

Remove a step.

Rename a button.

Delete a field nobody needs.

Those changes can genuinely improve a service.

But the same techniques can be used the other way around.

Make buying easy and canceling hard.

Make signing up effortless and account deletion difficult to find.

Highlight the advantages and hide important conditions behind a tiny asterisk.

The boundary isn't always dramatic.

Sometimes it's a meeting where someone says:

*We need more people to click here.*

That's a normal request.

Products need to work. Businesses need customers. Even free services sometimes need a way to pay for their servers.

The problem begins when the only thing we measure is how many clicks we got, rather than whether people understood what they were agreeing to.

**An interface can meet its business goals while failing the people who use it.**

And that's a little more uncomfortable to admit when you're one of the people building those interfaces.

## No is buried in a menu

I think you can learn a lot about an interface from the way it handles refusal.

Signing up?

A button on the homepage.

Unsubscribing?

Open settings, choose a section, find a submenu, explain why you're leaving, then confirm that you're absolutely sure.

Sometimes the “No” button feels like the prize at the end of a treasure hunt.

But being able to leave is also part of a good user experience.

A service that has good reasons for people to stay shouldn't have to hide the exit.

I'm not saying every action should take one click.

Deleting an account or canceling a service can have consequences. A confirmation can be useful.

But there's a difference between protecting someone from a mistake and exhausting them until they give up.

So the question isn't just: *can I refuse?*

It's also: **how much work does refusing take?**

## Habits we didn't choose

In the end, the most interesting part isn't the first click.

It's what happens afterward.

A notification setting becomes a regular interruption.

Autoplay turns one video into three.

One recommendation leads to another.

A saved preference starts to feel like a personal habit.

And after a while, we can't quite tell what we chose from what we simply left in place.

That's not a reason to distrust every button.

Defaults are necessary too.

Imagine an application asking you to choose the color of every link, the behavior of every menu, and the size of every icon before you can even see the homepage.

I'd uninstall it before the third question. 😅

A good default saves us from decisions we don't need to make.

A bad one takes advantage of the fact that we rarely question the settings already in place.

That's the difference.

## Who benefits from simplicity?

I like interfaces that don't make us think about every step.

That's a big part of what I try to build.

But I've become a little suspicious of journeys that feel remarkably smooth when they always lead in the same direction.

Buy.

Accept.

Subscribe.

Stay.

Simplicity should help us do what we came to do.

Not just what the service hoped we'd do.

I'll obviously keep clicking too quickly, accepting a window just to reach a page, and ignoring certain settings.

I haven't become a model reader of terms and conditions.

But there's one thing I'm trying to notice more often.

When an interface offers me two paths, I look at which one has been made easier.

And I ask myself a very simple question.

**Is this my choice, or just the one someone made for me?**
