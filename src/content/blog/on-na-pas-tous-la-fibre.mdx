A website loads instantly at the office.

Big screen.

Fast connection.

New computer.

Everything looks wonderful.

Then somebody opens it on an older phone, with a limited data plan and a network that changes throughout the day.

Suddenly that beautiful homepage becomes a patience test.

**The problem may not be the user's phone.**

It may be that we designed the service as though everyone used the same equipment as us.

## Coverage isn't the same as usage

We talk a lot about fibre and 5G when discussing digital progress.

Infrastructure matters.

But it doesn't explain who can actually use the Internet.

In a [December 2025 report](https://www.gsma.com/newsroom/press-release/new-gsma-report-shows-digital-reforms-could-connect-2-6-million-more-people-in-senegal-by-2030-and-expand-access-to-essential-services/), the GSMA reported that 4G covered around 97% of Senegal's population while actual mobile Internet usage remained much lower.

Affordability, device cost and digital skills were among the barriers highlighted.

That's a useful distinction: **being covered doesn't mean being connected, and being connected doesn't always mean a digital service is comfortable to use**.

## What a developer can control

I can't build new cell towers or make mobile data cheaper by myself.

But I can question what my app asks users to download.

Does that image really need several megabytes?

Do we need ten libraries to display a simple form?

Should a video load before the essential page content?

Every decision has a cost.

And when users pay it with data, battery life and waiting time, that cost is real.

## The real test isn't always at the office

Imagine an application form.

It works perfectly on a fast connection.

But the network drops before submission, and the user loses everything they typed.

They must start over.

Maybe they won't.

The most useful improvement might not be a smoother animation. It might be keeping form data, explaining the error clearly or allowing the submission to resume.

That's user experience too.

## Practical habits

For mobile-first services, I'd start with:

- Optimised images and lazy loading.
- Fewer unnecessary scripts.
- Testing on real phones.
- Clear loading and error states.
- Preserving form inputs when appropriate.
- Making essential actions work without heavy animations.
- Measuring page weight under slow-network conditions.

Some products need maps, video and real-time information. The point isn't to remove everything rich.

It's to make every resource earn its place.

## Inclusion is a design decision

If a public service, job application or business tool is almost impossible to use on a modest phone, people are excluded even when the service is technically online.

Better infrastructure helps.

So does better design.

I try to remember that **the machine I develop on isn't the world my product will live in**.

A genuinely fast website isn't one that impresses the developer.

It's one that lets the user complete their task.

Even when the network wasn't informed about our ambitions. 😅
