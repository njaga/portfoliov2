Picture a small business taking orders on WhatsApp.

One person writes the details in a notebook. Someone else copies them into a spreadsheet. Then a customer calls to ask about an order, and everyone starts searching.

You might think: **“They need an app!”**

Maybe. But before writing any code, it would help to understand why information moves through the business like that.

That's what interests me about digital transformation for small businesses. It isn't only a technology problem. It's also about processes, trust and habits that can be surprisingly hard to change.

## The first solution may not be software

Many small businesses already use phones, WhatsApp, spreadsheets and social media every day.

The issue is often how these tools connect—or don't.

Start with a few simple decisions: where orders are recorded, who updates them, how delays are flagged, and what information should be retrievable within a minute.

A clear process with a simple tool beats an elaborate application nobody opens.

## Find what actually wastes time

Before proposing a solution, I'd ask four questions:

- What task happens almost every day?
- Where does information get lost?
- What causes errors and delays?
- How will we know the change helped?

Those answers reveal a sensible first project.

For one business, that might be quotations. For another, invoicing, inventory or customer follow-up.

## Useful tools already exist

A complete business profile on Google can make it easier to find a location and opening hours.

WhatsApp Business can organise initial conversations, provided requests are tracked properly.

A shared spreadsheet, an invoicing service or a lightweight business tool can reduce duplicate entry.

A website is useful when it meets a real need: explaining an offer, building trust, receiving enquiries or publishing reliable information. I explore that further in [Why does a business still need a website?](/blog/importance-site-web-2025).

The right tool isn't necessarily the most impressive. It's the one that fits the business's real constraints: budget, connectivity, equipment, training and time.

## The part people forget: the people

A technically excellent system can still go unused.

If a team doesn't understand why its habits need to change, it will usually find its way back to the old process.

A successful rollout needs small, practical steps: demonstrate a real use case, train people, explain the benefit, listen to friction and adjust.

You don't digitally transform a business **against** the people who work there.

## Measure one small improvement

Forget the grand speeches for a moment.

If finding an invoice takes two minutes instead of twenty, that's progress.

If an order no longer needs to be copied three times, that's progress.

If a customer gets a clearer answer, that's progress too.

I prefer this gradual approach: **one specific problem, one understandable solution, one measurable result**.

Paper won't disappear overnight. And it doesn't always have to. What matters is whether the business works a little better tomorrow than it does today.
