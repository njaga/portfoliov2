You rarely notice how important an IT system is while it works.

An identity-card application. An administrative file. A check. A service that is supposed to simply respond.

Then one day, it stops.

And something that sounded like an issue only for IT teams becomes a very real problem for thousands of people.

That's what the incident reported by Senegal's **Directorate of File Automation (DAF)** in February 2026 reminds me of.

## What was officially confirmed

In its [February 5, 2026 statement](https://www.interieur.gouv.sn/actualites/communiques/communique-de-la-direction-de-l-automatisation-des-fichiers-du-05-fevrier-2026), Senegal's Interior Ministry said that an incident in the DAF system had temporarily suspended production of national identity cards.

The ministry reported technical recovery measures, stated that citizens' personal data remained intact and announced an investigation.

Those are the facts the institution publicly confirmed at that point.

Other reports relayed claims attributed to a cybercriminal group about allegedly stolen data. **A criminal claim is not, by itself, independent verification of what data was compromised or in what quantity.**

That distinction matters enormously when identity information is involved.

## Why an interruption reaches so far

An identity card isn't used only during an identity check.

It can be required for administrative, financial and professional processes, and sometimes travel.

When its production stops, the consequences reach far beyond the IT system responsible.

That's what makes public infrastructure critical: many other activities depend on it.

## What the incident teaches us

Cybersecurity is often described as the ability to stop intrusions.

That's necessary. But the next question is just as important: **what happens when normal operations are no longer possible?**

Teams need to isolate systems, understand what happened, preserve evidence, restore priority services and communicate clearly with the public.

Resilient public services need multiple safeguards:

- Access control and privileged-account management.
- Network segmentation and patch management.
- Tested backups and continuity plans.
- Monitoring and incident investigation.
- Recovery exercises.
- Clear communication during disruption.

Owning security tools isn't enough. They need to be part of how the organisation works.

## What about digital sovereignty?

Sovereignty is not only about where a server is located.

It also involves control over dependencies, providers, emergency procedures, local expertise and data recovery.

A locally hosted system can still be vulnerable. A well-designed system can still be fragile if nobody knows how to restore it.

What matters is an organisation's real ability to **understand, control and recover** its services.

## My takeaway

As a developer, cases like this remind me that invisible decisions—backups, permissions, documentation and monitoring—become very visible when a service stops.

No organisation can guarantee zero incidents.

But we can build systems that are better prepared to prevent, detect, respond to and recover from them.

And we can discuss cybersecurity without confusing confirmed facts, criminal claims and hypotheses.
