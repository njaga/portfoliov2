In a transport app, a customer taps one button.

They want a car.

In a delivery app, they tap another.

They want their order.

Simple, right?

From the screen, yes.

But behind one button can live **an entire organisation that needs to work in real time**.

It's the kind of complexity I've encountered while working on business systems and multi-service platforms.

## Simple interface, complicated system

Consider a hypothetical ride booking.

The customer confirms a request.

The system finds an available driver, may calculate a price, waits for acceptance, updates statuses and sends notifications.

What if the driver declines?

What if nobody is available?

What if the customer cancels just as the driver accepts?

What if the network fails after acceptance but before the confirmation arrives?

The customer doesn't want to understand all of this.

They just want to know whether the ride is confirmed.

**A good app hides complexity from the user, not from the engineers who need to manage it.**

## The importance of states

Business systems often depend on state transitions.

An order can be created, accepted, prepared, picked up, delivered or cancelled.

Those labels aren't just coloured tags.

They determine what people are allowed to do.

Can a customer cancel after pickup? Who confirms delivery? What happens when a booking is refused?

If these rules aren't explicit, the code can end up with contradictory conditions scattered everywhere.

Each new feature becomes harder.

## Notifications add another layer

The same event may matter to several people.

Customers need confirmations. Providers need requests. Administrators may need alerts.

The system must decide who receives what, when and where.

An event may be delivered twice. A notification can arrive when someone is offline. A booking may be cancelled while a message is still in a queue.

These aren't details people show off in a sales demo.

But they determine whether the service feels trustworthy.

## When features collide

A multi-service app might combine transport, delivery, bookings and commerce.

It's attractive: one account, several needs.

But each area has different actors, rules and exceptions.

Trying to push everything into one data model because the screens look similar can create a mess.

I prefer to separate shared services—accounts, authentication, notifications—from rules specific to each business area.

It can take longer initially.

It often saves trouble later.

## Start by drawing the journey

Before adding buttons, I try to map the scenarios.

What does the customer do? What does the provider do? Which statuses exist? Which transitions are forbidden? What happens on failure?

A simple diagram can expose a contradiction that several files of code might hide.

Only then does implementation begin.

## The quiet kind of elegance

We admire software for animations, maps and beautiful transitions.

I do too.

But there's a quieter elegance: a product that gives clear answers even when things don't go to plan.

Users don't need to know there are twenty rules behind a button.

They need those rules to work.

**Sometimes the best engineering makes complexity disappear for the people using the product.**

Unfortunately for the developer, the complexity doesn't actually disappear. 😅
