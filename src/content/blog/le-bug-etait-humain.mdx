The button works.

The database responds.

The tests pass.

And something is still wrong.

The client insists they asked for one thing. The developer says they delivered exactly what was agreed. The person using the product is looking for a feature nobody thought to build.

Three people.

Three versions of the same application.

And not a single error message to explain what happened.

**The bug wasn't in the code.**

## A working app can miss the point

Developers like problems we can reproduce.

A form crashes? Check the logs.

A query is slow? Measure it.

A button doesn't respond? Inspect it.

But what do we do when a feature works perfectly and solves the wrong problem?

Consider a fictional example: a company asks for a dashboard to monitor field operations.

We build attractive charts, advanced filters and colourful statistics.

Except the managers didn't really need charts.

What they needed at eight in the morning was a quick way to see which staff members were absent and who could replace them.

Technically, the application is a success.

It just answered the wrong question.

## "I thought that was obvious"

That sentence should set off a small alarm on every project.

*I thought that was obvious.*

That the customer would receive a notification.

That an administrator could edit an order.

That cancelling a booking would free the slot again.

That users would know what to do after clicking.

What seems obvious to one person can be completely invisible to another.

I've learned to be suspicious of the things nobody bothers to specify.

When I work on business applications, I try to ask questions that can sound almost silly.

Who performs this action?

What happens next?

And what if it fails?

**An awkward question before development usually costs less than an awkward correction after launch.**

## Silence is a decision too

Sometimes a team spots an issue and doesn't mention it immediately.

Nobody wants to delay the project.

Someone assumes another person has already noticed.

Everyone hopes it will sort itself out.

Then the product goes live.

And the small doubt nobody voiced becomes everybody's emergency.

This isn't always about a lack of expertise. Sometimes competent people work in environments where saying *I'm not sure* feels risky.

That's dangerous.

A team that cannot ask questions ends up paying a high price for its certainty.

## Developers cannot read minds

We sometimes describe a great developer as someone who instantly understands the client.

That would be convenient.

But a software project depends on knowledge that no single person has.

The business process belongs to the people doing the work. The technical constraints belong to the people building the system. Real-world usage often becomes clear only when people try it.

We have to bring those perspectives together.

Sketch a user journey.

Put an early version in someone's hands.

Write down what an action is supposed to trigger.

Discuss what happens when it doesn't.

And accept that a demo might challenge an idea we really liked.

I explored that hidden complexity in [The monster behind the screen](/blog/le-monstre-derriere-lecran).

## Fix the system, not just the blame

After an incident, the tempting question is: **whose fault was it?**

Sometimes responsibility really does need to be established.

But assigning blame alone won't prevent the next mistake.

It can be more useful to understand where information was lost, why an assumption wasn't checked or how a decision happened without the right people involved.

That doesn't mean removing accountability.

It means improving the process instead of stopping at a name.

## The next bug

I'll keep checking my queries, validations and permissions.

Of course I will.

But I'm also trying to test something less visible: whether we actually understand the same project.

A syntax error may take seconds to fix.

Several weeks of misunderstanding are another matter.

**Code cannot faithfully execute an intention nobody has clearly expressed.**

No framework is going to solve that for us.
