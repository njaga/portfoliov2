The workday is over.

In theory.

I close one computer, go home, and sometimes open another.

This time there are no colleagues waiting in a meeting. No job description deciding exactly what I should do next.

There are client requests, unfinished ideas, a personal project with a bug, and one simple question: **where do I start?**

Welcome to the second workday. 😅

## Two kinds of work, one brain

I work in digital communications. That role comes with deadlines, responsibilities, priorities and plenty of unexpected requests.

Outside that job, I'm building [KAMIT](https://kamit.tech), my small company, and personal products such as [KUMBA](https://kumba.media).

These activities overlap in software, content, design and marketing. But they are not the same.

At work, I have a clear responsibility to my employer.

With my own projects, I also have to decide **which problems are worth pursuing**. Nobody hands me a perfectly ordered task list.

That freedom is exciting.

It can also be exhausting when everything seems important.

## The productivity trap

A good idea arrives. I tell myself: *I'll just try something quickly.*

I open the project. Fix one detail. Notice another improvement. Then start rebuilding a whole component.

Hours later, I have been busy. But what have I actually achieved?

Software makes it easy to confuse activity with progress. Changing a screen feels satisfying. Choosing not to add a feature is harder.

I explore that problem in [One feature too many](/blog/la-fonction-de-trop).

## Deciding what matters

When projects compete for the same hours, I try to ask:

- **What have I promised?** Client and work deadlines are real commitments.
- **What creates the most value now?** Fixing a broken payment matters more than adjusting a card shadow.
- **What can wait?** A new idea isn't urgent simply because it is exciting.

I'd love to say I always follow this order.

I don't.

Sometimes I spend too much time on an interesting technical challenge and remember a less glamorous task is still waiting.

## Not every hour belongs to work

There is a quieter trap too.

When you love building things, every free moment can start looking like unused production capacity.

An evening? More code.

A weekend? A new website.

An idea during a walk? Open the task manager.

But fatigue isn't a bug you fix by importing a new library.

Rest, relationships and life away from screens aren't obstacles to productivity. They matter in their own right.

I haven't found a perfect balance. I'm just trying to respect that limit more consistently.

## Why keep going?

Because I love building.

Because personal projects let me experiment, make my own mistakes and discover what I want to work on over the long term.

And because growing something doesn't happen in a straight line.

Some days I improve a product. Other days I mostly learn what not to do.

What I want to avoid is sacrificing everything important just to feel busy everywhere.

**Two workdays don't give you twice the energy.**

They force you to be more deliberate with the energy you have.

I'm still learning how.
