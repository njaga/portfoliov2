**I went back to the company where I started as an intern.**

Back then, much of my day involved figuring out why a computer, a piece of software or a device had suddenly decided not to cooperate.

These days, I'm more likely to ask why a website isn't showing up in Google, how to explain a business in a few words, or how to communicate consistently across Senegal, Côte d'Ivoire and Sierra Leone.

Come to think of it, my work hasn't changed *that* much.

**Something isn't working. I try to understand why. Then I try to make it better.**

The problems have simply grown a little. 😅

On **May 4, 2026**, I returned to VIGILUS as **Head of Digital Communications**.

It wasn't exactly a return to square one.

To explain why, let's rewind.

## 2022: hello, IT support speaking

I joined VIGILUS as an IT support intern on **January 15, 2022**.

IT support has a wonderful characteristic: nobody thinks about you when everything works. When a computer refuses to start, however, you become a very popular person.

At that stage, I wasn't presenting five-year brand strategies. I was helping colleagues, troubleshooting problems, maintaining equipment and learning how the company's software worked.

Support taught me something I still rely on: **the problem someone describes is not always the problem you actually need to solve**.

“The app doesn't work” can mean a login issue, an unclear workflow, a missing record or a broken connection.

Before reaching for the keyboard, you have to listen.

I didn't know it yet, but that habit would follow me into almost every role I held afterward.

The more I understood VIGILUS from the inside, the more opportunities I noticed.

Websites to create. Repetitive tasks to simplify. Internal tools that could make life easier.

I had joined to fix computers.

I was already thinking about fixing whole processes.

Not ideal if you're hoping for quiet workdays. 😂

## The developer who ended up talking about real estate

My role gradually expanded into **digital project management**.

I worked on websites, applications, internal tools and digitalisation projects, learning to bring technical and business needs together.

Then came a job I hadn't originally imagined: **Digital Manager at Carré d'Or**, the real-estate business that later became **VIGILUS Properties**.

Going from IT support to property marketing sounds like a strange detour.

It turned out to be an excellent lesson.

Consider a property listing.

As a developer, you might be pleased: the page loads, the gallery works and the contact form saves enquiries.

Technically, excellent.

But if the photos aren't convincing, the location is unclear or visitors don't know how to arrange a viewing, the website might work perfectly while **failing at its actual job**.

Real estate taught me to see a page through the eyes of someone discovering an offer for the first time.

How do you present a property? Build trust? Make the next step obvious?

I worked on platforms, content, visuals, brand identity and online presence.

I was still a developer in the way I approached problems.

But I was starting to understand that beautiful code doesn't pay the bills by itself. 😄

## “Okay, but why would a customer buy it?”

My first chapter at VIGILUS also included responsibilities in **marketing and sales**.

For a developer, that can be a healthy reality check.

You can spend hours admiring what a product can do.

Then comes a much shorter question:

**“What difference does it make for the customer?”**

Ouch.

I learned to talk less about features and more about real needs. To understand the Group's business lines, what potential customers expect and why they might choose one provider over another.

Private security, facility management, property services and technical solutions aren't bought like apps from a store.

People need clarity, trust and evidence that you understand their constraints.

Marketing also taught me something about SEO: **being visible isn't enough; you have to be found by the right person with the right answer**.

By then I'd worked in IT, development, design, marketing and sales.

You might think I had enough hats.

Naturally, I went looking for a few more.

## 2024: leaving was not a holiday

I left VIGILUS in **May 2024**.

That ended the first chapter, not my appreciation for what I'd learned there.

I wanted to tackle other problems and build software that people depended on in their daily work.

At **PROSEN**, where I worked in IT management, things became very practical.

I helped build and maintain business tools including **JOTALI** for attendance and patrols, **DOOLÉ** for HR and scheduling, invoicing tools, and **Traxelio** for tracking field operations.

Product demos are often lovely.

The data is clean. Everybody clicks the correct button. Nobody loses their connection.

Then real life begins.

A manager needs a schedule. Someone needs to record attendance. Another person needs a document right now.

In those moments, nobody cares how elegant your architecture is if the essential feature doesn't work.

That experience taught me more about real users, permissions, reliability, maintenance and all the cases that don't fit into a perfect demo.

I also continued building **[KAMIT Digital Solutions](https://kamit.tech)**, my own company, around a persistent idea: useful software should solve real problems.

In short, I left with a toolbox.

The next two years taught me just how many things were missing from it.

## 2026: familiar faces, a very different challenge

In the spring of 2026, former colleagues and a former VIGILUS executive got back in touch.

A return became a real possibility.

What interested me wasn't just coming back to a familiar company. It was the chance to contribute with a different perspective and a much broader scope.

On **May 4, 2026**, I was back.

This time, it wasn't about one website, one application or one subsidiary.

It was about helping build a coherent digital presence across **several brands and countries**.

Senegal. Côte d'Ivoire. Sierra Leone. Mobility. Properties. Security and business services.

My developer brain had a sensible thought: *we're going to need a very good system for organising all this.*

For once, it was absolutely right.

## Security in the morning, real estate in the afternoon

The most interesting part of my job is also what makes it funny to describe.

One part of my work may concern **corporate guarding**. Another might involve **fire safety**, **technical maintenance**, **car rentals**, **real estate** or **payment equipment**.

Try fitting that into one sentence at a family dinner.

Good luck. 😅

But the variety has taught me to treat each business as its own world.

Someone looking for security officers at an industrial site doesn't have the same questions as a traveller renting a car in Dakar.

A person searching for a furnished apartment shouldn't have to read three pages about the Group's structure before seeing available properties.

So I've worked with the teams to make our digital presence clearer, more useful and easier to discover.

That includes **[VIGILUS Facilities](https://vigilus-facilities.com)** for services in Senegal; **[VIGILUS Group](https://groupevigilus.com)** and **[VIGILUS Côte d'Ivoire](https://groupevigilus.ci)** for corporate information and local offerings; **[VIGILUS Sierra Leone](https://vigilus-facilities.sl)** for the English-language market; **[VIGILUS Properties](https://vigilus-properties.sn)** for real estate; and **[VIGILUS Mobility](https://locationvoituredakar.com)** for vehicle rental.

Of course, websites are only part of the work.

There are campaigns, brochures, newsletters, videos, event materials and countless small corrections that will never make a glamorous LinkedIn post.

A broken link. An unclear form. A poor image. A page nobody can find in search. A sentence that needs to be rewritten three times before it actually means something.

Digital communication isn't only about finding a clever slogan.

It's making sure **the experience behind the slogan lives up to what it promises**.

## Eight websites, two apps… and an impressive number of browser tabs

In my first progress report to management in **June 2026**, our work already included **eight redesigned websites and two revamped applications**.

There were also brochures to update or create, event materials, campaigns and video projects.

Written down, it looks wonderfully organised.

In reality, a redesigned website isn't a folder you close forever.

Content changes. User journeys need simplifying. Search performance needs attention. New offers appear.

And sometimes you finish a page, take another look and finally think of a better way to explain it.

That's useful.

It's also an excellent way to make sure the work never feels quite finished. 😄

One lesson stayed with me: **the number of deliverables matters less than what they help people do**.

Can a visitor understand the service? Find what they need? Contact the right team?

That's the real test.

## Freetown: when digital work steps outside the screen

In **July 2026**, I was in **Freetown** for the official opening of **VIGILUS Sierra Leone**.

It was a defining moment in this second chapter.

Suddenly, many tasks that normally lived in separate browser tabs converged around a single event: the website, brand visuals, English-language content, institutional video, photography, event coverage and social-media storytelling.

An opening ceremony has one inconvenient difference from a website project:

**You can't ask the guests to come back after the next deployment.**

The materials have to be ready. The images have to be usable. The message has to be consistent. And the work needs to capture what actually happens.

I was reminded of something I learned in IT support: when the moment arrives, a good theoretical plan isn't enough. Things have to work.

In Freetown, digital communication was no longer just what people saw on a screen.

It was part of the experience happening around us.

## What did I actually learn?

I could end with an impressive sentence about cross-functional synergies and digital transformation.

But I've already spent enough time removing sentences like that from brochures. 😂

The lessons I value are more straightforward.

**IT support taught me to listen before fixing things.**

**Development taught me to structure the problem before writing code.**

**Real estate taught me to see a website from the customer's point of view.**

**Sales taught me that features don't sell themselves.**

**Business software taught me that reliability matters more than a beautiful demo.**

**And multi-country communication keeps teaching me how to balance a common identity with local realities.**

I still make mistakes, learn from colleagues and change my mind when there's a better way to do something.

But I understand more clearly why all those seemingly unrelated jobs ended up pointing in the same direction.

## Back where I started. Not back to who I was.

Physically, yes, I've returned to a familiar organisation.

Professionally, not at all.

The intern who tried to get a computer working hasn't disappeared. He's simply become someone who also tries to make websites, products, campaigns and teams work together.

I left in 2024.

I came back in 2026.

And maybe that's the simplest way to explain it:

**I joined VIGILUS to solve IT problems. I returned to solve problems that are a little bigger.**

Computers probably still break down, of course.

Luckily, I'm no longer necessarily the first person people call. 😉
